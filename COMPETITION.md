## Overview
Welcome to Homework 3 of the UCU Artificial Intelligence 2026 course
This is an NLP homework assignment for the UCU AI 2026 class. Your task is to build a model that classifies a text as written by a human, generated by an LLM (ai), or a fusion of both (a human text partially rewritten by an LLM). Texts are in English and German and come from news, scientific papers, blogs, parliamentary debates, court cases, student essays, and books.
The training set has 272,352 labeled texts and the test set has 25,000. Predict a label for every row in `test.csv`. The metric is macro-F1 (see Evaluation).

### What to submit
* Kaggle: you can make up to 5 submissions per day; select up to two final submissions.
* LMS: reproducible code (notebooks or scripts) and a technical report describing your methodology, experiments (including unsuccessful ones), and final model. A notebook with outputs does not replace the report. The report must be 1–2 pages of text (tables and figures may go in an appendix); longer reports lose points.
* One-on-one defense: walk us through your approach, report, and code.

Start early, and begin with a simple pipeline that produces a valid submission.

### Rules
* No proprietary LLMs. Do not use closed models through an API or chat interface (e.g. GPT, Claude, Gemini) to label, generate, or extract features from the data. Open-weight models (e.g. XLM-R, mDeBERTa, Llama, Qwen) are allowed.
* No external data to improve the score. The dataset is built from a public source. Using it, or any other external information, to look up or improve test predictions is prohibited. Train on `train.csv` only; pretrained model weights are allowed.
* Data leakage costs points. For example: fitting vectorizers, scalers, or feature selection on validation or test rows, or using test labels in any form.
* Independent work. Sharing code between participants results in 0 points for the homework. Cite external code and ideas.
* AI tools may be used for writing code, but not for writing the report.

### Compute
* Colab free tier: T4/L4 GPU (12–16 GB), about 12 hours per session. Kaggle: up to 30 GPU hours per week.
* You do not need the full training set: a 20–50k subsample is enough for a transformer. Keep the sample fixed when you compare models.
* EDA, tokenization checks, and classical models run fine on CPU.

### Grading (10 pts + 1 bonus)
| Part | Points | What we expect |
|---|---|---|
| Private leaderboard | 2 | Normalized to 0.5–2.0 by the final private leaderboard standing. |
| EDA | 1 | Class balance by language and domain, text lengths, examples of each class, and how these inform your choices. What makes fusion different? |
| Baseline | 1 | Vectorizers (e.g. TF-IDF word/char n-grams) with classical ML models, a small parameter search, per-class metrics, and a confusion matrix. |
| Encoder | 1 | A fine-tuned multilingual transformer (e.g. XLM-R, mDeBERTa), handling of texts longer than the maximum length, and a comparison with the baseline on the same split. |
| Your approach | 2 | At least two further experiments, each compared with a fixed reference: long-text handling, other backbones, perplexity-based features, stylometry, ensembles, class weights or thresholds for macro-F1, etc. |
| Report and defense | 3 | A 1–2 page report: methodology, results including failed attempts, the final model with run instructions. Plus your ability to explain your work. Reports longer than 2 pages lose points. |

The defense can lower the points for any part, including the leaderboard, if you cannot explain or reproduce your work. Use macro-F1 on a fixed validation split for all comparisons. Meaningful experiments earn credit even if they do not improve the score. Points are deducted for long unfiltered printouts, runtime errors, and unstructured code.

## Evaluation
Submissions are evaluated using the macro-averaged F1 score over the three classes human, ai, and fusion.

Each class contributes equally regardless of how frequent it is, so the hardest class, `fusion`, matters as much as the other two. Higher scores correspond to better predictions.
The public leaderboard uses 30% of the test data (7,501 rows). The remaining 70% (17,499 rows) determine the private leaderboard and final standings. Submit predictions for all 25,000 rows in `test.csv`; their public/private membership is not provided.
Use macro-F1 for your internal validation as well (`sklearn.metrics.f1_score(y_true, y_pred, average="macro")`). The leaderboard contributes 2 of the 10 homework points; implementation, experiments, the report, and the one-on-one discussion are evaluated separately as described in Overview.
